Policy & economics

An entire political system, designed so the parts don’t contradict each other

There is an old genre where an author works out a complete coherent system — the constitution, the economy, the foreign policy, the safety net — and checks that the pieces fit. The UCN papers are that exercise written in policy-research register rather than fiction, anchored to an explicit macroeconomic model, and closed by a roadmap that admits what it would require and does not have.

What it is

Eight numbered papers covering constitution, economy, defence, IP, drug policy and foreign relations — every claim sourced, every incompatibility with current law acknowledged in the roadmap.

The series is eight numbered papers — Framework, Sovereignty, Defence, Social, IP, Constitution, Foreign Policy, Roadmap — plus an economic backbone in the Energy-Resource Economic Model, a sovereign-digital-currency analysis, a constitutional-law reading of the Australian status quo, and a supporting two-part study of Western nuclear procurement failure. The design constraint is internal consistency: the drug policy has to be affordable under the economics, the defence posture has to be consistent with the foreign policy.

The distinguishing moves are specific rather than gestural: a modular constitution with version-control semantics and tiered amendment thresholds; an EREM-backed currency with an AUD 100 M personal wealth ceiling; citizen-only residential property and direct share ownership; a Public Knowledge Mandate with no embargo period; a minimal deterrent capped at ten warheads alongside a year of non-weapons national service; government-manufactured pharmaceutical-grade recreational drugs under Portuguese-style decriminalisation; and 185 non-aggression pacts derived from the Correlates of War dataset with no trade partner above 20% of goods exports.

Every claim carries a citation — the roughly 80% fall in Portuguese overdose deaths over twenty years, El Salvador’s 92% Bitcoin non-use figure, the Atlantic Council’s 137-jurisdiction CBDC tracker. A separate strand runs to a companion folder on an optional Crown confederation of Australia, Canada and the United Kingdom, in which the Crown performs defined governance functions rather than serving as a shared symbol.

The roadmap is what makes this policy research rather than a manifesto. The series ends by listing what its own proposals would require and do not have: referenda, elections, international consent, and compatibility with treaty frameworks it currently contradicts. A design document that costs its own political feasibility honestly is doing something a platform never does.

Methods

How it works

  • Whole-system consistency. Papers written so proposals cannot contradict each other — the constraint that makes the exercise hard.
  • Version-controlled constitution. Tier 1/2/3 amendment thresholds, so different provisions carry different change costs.
  • EREM as macroeconomic backbone. Currency and wealth policy grounded in an explicit energy-and-resource model rather than left implicit.
  • Sourced evidence anchors. Every empirical claim tied to a citation — Portuguese drug policy, CBDC adoption, procurement data.
Stated limitations

What it does not do

Taken from the folder’s own README. Nothing here has been softened.

  • Explicitly policy research, not enacted law. Nothing here has legal standing anywhere.
  • Many proposals contradict current Australian law and NPT, Five Eyes and IMF frameworks. The roadmap paper says so directly.
  • A ten-warhead deterrent is incompatible with Australia’s NPT obligations as a non-nuclear-weapon state.
  • The roadmap acknowledges the proposal would need referenda, elections and international consent it does not have.
  • A designed system’s internal coherence says nothing about whether it would survive contact with actual politics.
